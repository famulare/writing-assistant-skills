## Lead finding — the opener (author-flagged)

Current opener:

> "I fell down a rabbit hole in the 1949 literature last week and I can't stop
> thinking about it. Short version: we treat the three poliovirus serotypes as a
> hard fact of nature — type 1, type 2, type 3, clean boxes — and part of that
> hardness is something we manufactured in the 1950s."

| Passage | Failure mode | Why it matters | Severity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| "I fell down a rabbit hole in the 1949 literature last week" | generic blog-opener cliché | "fell down a rabbit hole" is one of the most worn openings on the internet; it signals *personal-blog throat-clear*, not the specific, load-bearing claim this author makes. It also spends the highest-value real estate (first thing heard aloud) on the author's process rather than the argument. | high |
| "and I can't stop thinking about it" | stakes-narration | Announces that the topic is interesting instead of demonstrating it. The card's rule is the opposite move: "a blunt title or first sentence does the work." The title already does the work ("We decided polio comes in three flavors. The old data weren't so sure."), so this clause is pure told-not-shown significance. | high |
| "Short version:" | throat-clearing / sign-posting | Labels the structural move ("here comes the summary") rather than making the point. It delays the real thesis by a third clause and reads as a verbal tic when heard aloud. The compressed thesis should just *be* the first thing, unlabeled. | med |

**Why the paragraph reads weak aloud (root cause):** the real argument — *serotype
hardness is partly manufactured* — is the third beat, buried behind two throat-clears
and a label. Against the casual card's "compressed thesis opener that names the real
argument," the opener currently *delays and muffles* the argument it should lead with.
The title carries the thesis; the first sentence then repeats the throat-clear posture
instead of driving straight into the claim, so the mouth-feel is soft: three warm-up
phrases before anything lands.

**Direction for the fix (not replacement prose):**
- Cut all three tells ("fell down a rabbit hole," "I can't stop thinking about it,"
  "Short version:"). None carries content; each is a generic blog reflex.
- Open on the manufactured-hardness claim itself — the "we treat the serotypes as a
  hard fact of nature … and part of that hardness is something we manufactured"
  sentence is already the compressed thesis. Promote it to first position so the
  strongest, most specific line is what the ear hits first.
- The genuine self-disclosure (that this came from reading the 1949 papers) is a
  legitimate casual move and can stay — but as *texture after the claim lands*, not as
  the muffling first beat. Reading old tables is the evidence for the claim, not a
  substitute for stating it.
- Test aloud: the first sentence should be sayable in one breath and should already
  contain the argument, so a listener who stops after sentence one still has the point.

## Em-dash function test

Density is moderate and most dashes are load-bearing — keep:
- "hard fact of nature — type 1, type 2, type 3, clean boxes — and part of…" (appositive with internal commas; only the dash pair works).
- "The one that got me is 'Per' — reportedly from…" (concrete instance dropped into the flow).
- "…we assume between-serotype viruses are unfit — so the people running the typing assays have never seen one in their lives." (genuine turn/landing).
- "…kept them looking sharp — and we just removed one more." (closing punch; earns it).

One dash to fix, not for density but for grammar: the "and — before we built the
assays…" dash in "How we lost the plot" (see table above). The nested dash inside the
Houston parenthetical ("…paralysis more visible — poorer places like Houston…") is
contrastive and readable, but it stacks a dash inside a parenthetical; acceptable in
casual register, worth a glance if the paragraph feels busy aloud.
