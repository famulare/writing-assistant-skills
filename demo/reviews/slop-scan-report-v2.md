## Part 1 — The opener (§1 "The partition we trust")

Full text under scrutiny:

> We sort polioviruses into three serotypes and treat that partition as a hard fact of the virus. It is a useful fact, real enough to build vaccines on. But some of its hardness is manufactured. It is partly an artifact of how the field standardized its assays in the early 1950s. Read the literature from before that standardization and the picture is less black and white than the dogma today. They knew, way back, that serotypes were fuzzy; we are only surprised now because developments since the 1950s had largely defined away the evidence for it.

### Finding O1 — Concession-before-thesis softening (HIGH)

- **Span:** "It is a useful fact, real enough to build vaccines on."
- **Tell:** disclaimer-before-conviction. This is exactly the sentence the author flagged. The paragraph's real move is the turn — *the partition is partly an artifact* — and this sentence spends a full beat conceding ground *before* the turn arrives. The card's close/open rule ("conviction over disclaimer") is violated at the open: the reader hears the hedge first and the thesis second.
- **Why it reads weak aloud:** the concession lands with equal sentence weight to the thesis, so the ear can't tell which one the paragraph is about. The "But..." in the next sentence has to fight uphill against a concession that was given its own standalone sentence.
- **Direction:** don't delete the concession's *content* (the "real enough to build vaccines on" point is genuine and does epistemic work — it's why the artifact claim is interesting, not a debunking). But it should be subordinated to the thesis, not co-equal with it, or moved after the turn lands. Let the thesis punch first; let the concession qualify it, not precede it.

### Finding O2 — Buried / double-hedged lead (HIGH)

- **Span:** "But some of its hardness is manufactured. It is partly an artifact of how the field standardized its assays in the early 1950s."
- **Tell:** the actual thesis is split across two sentences and each is independently hedged — "**some** of its hardness" and "**partly** an artifact." Two soft quantifiers on the load-bearing claim, back to back, diffuse the punch. This is the core of the "weak aloud" complaint: the sentence that should be the hammer is padded on both sides.
- **Why it matters:** the calibrated claim is defensible and should stay calibrated (the piece is honest that the partition *is* mostly real). But one hedge carries the calibration; two reads as flinching. The reader can't hear a thesis being asserted, only a claim being qualified.
- **Direction:** consolidate the thesis into one sentence that states the artifact claim with conviction and carries a *single* calibrating word, rather than two half-strength sentences each carrying one. Keep the calibration; lose the doubling.

### Finding O3 — Warm-up first sentence / test the ¶1 lead (MED)

- **Span:** "We sort polioviruses into three serotypes and treat that partition as a hard fact of the virus."
- **Tell:** the card's "the real opener is often the second sentence/paragraph" test. Sentence 1 is scene-setting — it establishes the shared belief so it can be knocked down. That is a legitimate setup, but combined with the O1 concession sentence, the paragraph spends its first two sentences (roughly 30 words) before the thesis fires. For a piece read aloud, that's a long runway.
- **Note:** ¶1 cannot be cut wholesale — unlike the card's typical case, the thesis lives *inside* ¶1, not in ¶2. §2 ("This is not a new worry dressed up as history") cannot stand as the lead. So the fix is *internal compression* of ¶1, not deletion.
- **Direction:** tighten the setup so the tension (treated-as-hard-fact vs. partly-manufactured) arrives faster — ideally the reader hits the turn within the first sentence or two, not the third or fourth. Consider fusing the setup and the turn so the concession is a subordinate clause rather than a standalone sentence.

### Finding O4 — Generic idiom "less black and white" (MED)

- **Span:** "the picture is less black and white than the dogma today"
- **Tell:** clichéd idiom in a plain-technical voice, and a slight category mismatch — a "picture" is being compared to a "dogma" (image vs. belief). "Black and white" is exactly the kind of stock phrase the card's plain-technical register avoids; the piece elsewhere earns its vividness with mechanism ("defined away the evidence," "cleaned up"), so this idiom reads as filler by contrast.
- **Direction:** replace with the concrete thing the sentence means — the pre-standardization literature shows the serotypes as graded/overlapping rather than cleanly partitioned. Name the fuzziness directly (the piece's own word) rather than reaching for the idiom.

### Opener verdict

The author's ear is right. The weakness is not one bad word — it is **structural sequencing**: setup → concession → hedged-thesis (split) → thesis-restated. The conviction is there in the content but arrives fourth in line and doubly hedged. Fixing O1 and O2 (subordinate the concession, consolidate the thesis to one calibrated sentence) will do most of the work; O3/O4 are secondary tightening. Do not over-correct into false certainty — the calibration is real and defensible; the goal is *one* clean hedge carried by a thesis that lands first, not zero hedges.

### Em-dash function test

Density is acceptable — roughly one dash or pair per paragraph, not stacked. Spot check:

- §3 "The 'Per' strain — reportedly isolated from a three-year-old girl who died in West Virginia in 1940, and autopsied by Albert Sabin — is fully protected..." — load-bearing appositive (aside contains commas; main clause resumes). **Keep.**
- §5 "seasonal waves of a single serotype — at least in wealthier communities, where the age of infection was older..." — load-bearing qualification the clause resumes from. **Keep.**
- §5 "Per could be exactly such a virus — one that immunizes against type 1 and a bit against type 2." — appositive with a punch. **Keep.**
- §6 close "...made it look — and we are now removing one of the forces that kept it looking sharp." — contrastive turn landing the piece. **Keep.**

No substitutable em-dashes found. No action needed.

### Close (§6) — conviction check

The final sentence ("The partition was always fuzzier than the reference panel made it look — and we are now removing one of the forces that kept it looking sharp.") leads with conviction and lands the bookend against the opener's "surprised now." This is a strong close and satisfies the card's "conviction over disclaimer at a close." **Preserve.**
